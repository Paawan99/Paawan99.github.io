# Which Account First: FHSA vs. RRSP vs. TFSA

tags: tax, benefits, newcomer

## This is a general framework, not personalized advice

Canada's three main registered savings accounts — the FHSA, RRSP, and TFSA — each have different tax treatment and goals. There's no single "best" order that fits everyone; the right choice depends on your income, homeownership plans, employer benefits, and time horizon. This note is general education, not a recommendation for your specific situation — consider speaking with a licensed financial advisor for personalized advice.

## Consider FHSA first if you're planning to buy a first home

If you're a first-time home buyer (or expect to be one within the next few years) and can afford to set money aside, the First Home Savings Account is often considered first because contributions are tax-deductible and qualifying withdrawals toward a home are tax-free — a combination neither the RRSP nor TFSA offers alone. Because FHSA room only starts accumulating after you open the account (unlike the TFSA), opening one early — even with a small deposit — can be worthwhile if you might buy a home eventually.

## Consider RRSP to capture an employer match or when income is high

If your employer offers to match RRSP contributions, contributing at least enough to get the full match is often considered a priority, since it's effectively an immediate return on your contribution. The RRSP also tends to be more attractive when your current income (and marginal tax rate) is relatively high, since the deduction is worth more now, with the expectation you'll withdraw it later in retirement at a potentially lower tax rate.

## Consider TFSA for flexibility or lower income

If your income is relatively low or moderate, the immediate tax deduction from an RRSP is worth less, so some people prioritize the TFSA, where withdrawals are always tax-free and don't affect income-tested benefits or credits (RRSP/RRIF withdrawals count as taxable income, which can reduce benefits like the GST/HST credit or CCB). The TFSA is also useful for shorter-term goals or emergency funds because you can withdraw anytime without tax consequences.

## Putting it together

A common general approach is: capture any employer RRSP match, prioritize the FHSA if buying a first home is a near-term goal, then split additional savings between RRSP (especially in higher-income years) and TFSA (for flexibility and lower-income years) based on your own tax situation. This is only a starting framework — your ideal mix depends on personal factors this note can't account for, so treat it as general education rather than a personal financial plan.
